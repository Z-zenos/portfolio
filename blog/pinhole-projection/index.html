From 3D Point to Pixel: Which Drone Photos See the Spot You Clicked?

2026-10-09 · 13 min read

  • computer vision
  • 3d
  • geometry

You spot a crack on a 3D model. Finding the matching view among 500 drone photos takes more work. I built a feature for a drone inspection platform that connects the two: click a point on the model or point cloud, and a panel ranks candidate photos with a marker at the projected location. The math is small enough to fit in one function. Getting the axes, lens assumptions and meaning of “in view” right takes more care. Here is how I worked through it.

One point, many photos

For each photo we know where the camera was and which way it was pointing (its pose), and we have its field of view or camera calibration. For the clicked point P we know its 3D position. The question for each photo is: if I trace a straight line from P into this camera, does it land inside the image rectangle, and on which pixel? Run that test against every photo, keep the hits, sort them. A hit means the point is inside the viewing frustum; it may still be hidden behind something.

The pinhole model

Picture a closed box with a tiny hole in the front. Light from every point in the scene travels in a straight line through the hole and lands on the back wall. I approximate the lens as a central projection: scene rays pass through one camera centre. Drawing a virtual image plane in front of that centre keeps the picture upright. Its distance f sets the projection scale, or focal length; later I express it in pixels.

Because the small triangle and the big triangle are similar, the offset on the image is:

Δu = f · X / Z

That division by depth is the entire trick, and it explains two things you already know intuitively. Things further away look smaller: the same 20 m offset at 200 m lands half as far from the centre as at 100 m. And a single photo cannot tell you depth: every point along one ray has the same X/Z and Y/Z, so they all hit the same pixel. I need the clicked point’s 3D position from another source: here, a model built by structure from motion.

World to camera

The formula above needs X, Y, Z measured from the camera, along the camera's own axes. We start with world positions and an orientation. Two steps connect them to the projection formula.

Step 1: subtract. Express the clicked point and camera centre in the same local East/North/Up (ENU) frame, in metres, and subtract: d = P − C. Align reconstruction coordinates to that frame first, including scale and height datum. Use the camera centre, not an uncorrected navigation-antenna position. The result might be “20 m East, 30 m North and 100 m below the camera”.

Step 2: rotate into the camera frame. "20 m East" still does not tell you whether the point is on the left or the right of the photo; that depends on which way the camera faces. It is like giving directions to someone: "the tree is 20 m East" becomes "the tree is 20 m to your right" only once you know they are facing North.

I used a common computer-vision camera convention:

  • x points to the right of the image,
  • y points down the image,
  • z points forward, along the viewing direction.

Image pixel rows also count downwards from the top edge, so camera y and pixel v grow in the same direction. That gives the projection one less sign flip to get wrong.

Each pose stores its orientation as a unit quaternion q = [w, x, y, z] that rotates vectors from camera axes to ENU. Our backend converted gimbal angles or reconstruction output into that convention once. For body-relative gimbal angles, compose aircraft attitude and camera mounting rotation too; reconstruction poses may already be world-to-camera. Quaternions avoid an Euler-angle singularity in the frontend, but cannot repair an incorrect pose conversion. To go the other way, ENU into camera, you apply the inverse rotation, which for a unit quaternion is just its conjugate [w, −x, −y, −z]. Equivalently: take the dot product of d with the camera's right, down and forward axes.

Pitfall: write the direction of every rotation into its name ("q rotates camera → ENU"). Using the forward rotation where you need the inverse generally gives the wrong direction, not a simple mirror. The straight-down, North-up rotation here is its own inverse, so that test alone cannot catch the mistake. Include an oblique pose.

Finding focal length

With the axes settled, the lens sets the image scale. When calibrated pixel focal lengths are unavailable, I estimate them from a horizontal field of view fovH or a 35 mm-equivalent focal length. The ray that grazes the right edge of the image sits W/2 pixels from the centre, at depth f, and makes an angle of fovH/2 with the optical axis. Basic trigonometry gives:

f = (W / 2) / tan(fovH / 2)        // focal length in pixels

For a 4000 × 3000 image with a 90° horizontal field of view, f = 2000 / tan 45° = 2000 px. For square pixels, one f serves both axes. Scale the tangent, not the angle: the vertical half-angle is atan(1500 / 2000) ≈ 36.9°, not (90°/2) × 3/4 = 33.75°.

Pitfall: “35 mm-equivalent” uses the diagonal of a 36 × 24 mm frame. For square pixels, f_px = f_35 · hypot(W, H) / hypot(36, 24), with f_35 in millimetres. Using 36 mm as the equivalent width is only right for 3:2 images. For a synthetic 4000 × 3000 image at 24 mm equivalent, it gives 73.7° horizontally instead of 71.6°, shifting a true edge point inward by about 77 px. This assumes the metadata describes the delivered crop.

Metres to pixels

Assuming a centred principal point and no distortion, the camera coordinates (x, y, z) become:

u = W/2 + f · x / z        // column, from the left edge
v = H/2 + f · y / z        // row, from the top edge

I measure continuous image coordinates from the top-left outer corner, so pixel centres are at half-integers. Calibration that places the first pixel centre at (0, 0) needs a half-pixel conversion. Rotating, cropping or resizing a displayed photo also requires transforming the marker coordinates.

A worked example with round numbers. A drone hovers 100 m above flat ground, camera pointing straight down, top of the photo facing North. The image is 4000 × 3000 px, fovH = 90°, so f = 2000. The clicked point P is on the ground 20 m East and 30 m North of the spot directly below the drone.

  1. Subtract: d = (20, 30, −100) in (East, North, Up).
  2. Rotate: for this pose, camera right = East, camera down = South (top of image is North), camera forward = down to the ground. So x = 20, y = −30 (30 m North is 30 m "up the image"), z = 100.
  3. Project: u = 2000 + 2000 · 20/100 = 2400, v = 1500 + 2000 · (−30/100) = 900.

Sanity check with intuition first: the point is East and North, so it should land right of centre and above centre. Pixel (2400, 900) does exactly that, because v counts down from the top. Another way to see it: at 100 m depth, f / z = 20 px per metre on the ground, so 20 m East is 400 px right and 30 m North is 600 px up.

Behind the camera

The formula happily returns a pixel for a point behind the camera. Flip the example so the point is 100 m above the drone: z = −100, and the division flips both signs, putting a perfectly valid-looking dot at (1600, 2100), mirrored across the centre. So the rule is: reject when z ≤ 0, before dividing. A nadir (straight-down) dataset may never exercise this branch; an oblique photo can put a clicked point behind the camera.

Here is the projection test, written fresh for this post. Validate finite inputs once at ingestion: positive image dimensions, 0 < fovHDeg < 180, and a normalised quaternion in the declared order. The displacement d is P − C in ENU metres.

type Vec3 = [number, number, number];
type Quat = [number, number, number, number]; // [w, x, y, z], camera -> ENU

const cross = (a: Vec3, b: Vec3): Vec3 =>
  [a[1] * b[2] - a[2] * b[1], a[2] * b[0] - a[0] * b[2], a[0] * b[1] - a[1] * b[0]];

// Unit quaternion: v' = v + 2w(r×v) + 2r×(r×v), r = [x, y, z]
function rotate([w, x, y, z]: Quat, v: Vec3): Vec3 {
  const t = cross([x, y, z], v);
  const tt = cross([x, y, z], t);
  return [0, 1, 2].map((i) => v[i] + 2 * w * t[i] + 2 * tt[i]) as Vec3;
}

function project(d: Vec3, q: Quat, fovHDeg: number, W: number, H: number) {
  const [w, x, y, z] = q;
  const [cx, cy, cz] = rotate([w, -x, -y, -z], d); // ENU -> camera (inverse)
  if (!(cz > 0)) return null;                      // behind, at centre plane, or NaN
  const f = W / 2 / Math.tan((fovHDeg * Math.PI) / 360);
  const u = W / 2 + (f * cx) / cz;
  const v = H / 2 + (f * cy) / cz;
  if (!Number.isFinite(u) || !Number.isFinite(v)) return null;
  if (u < 0 || u >= W || v < 0 || v >= H) return null; // outside the frame
  return { u, v };
}

// Nadir, top of image = North: q = [0, 1, 0, 0] (180° about East)
project([20, 30, -100], [0, 1, 0, 0], 90, 4000, 3000); // { u: 2400, v: 900 }

Note that fovHDeg * Math.PI / 360 is "half the angle, in radians" in one step. The half-open bounds [0, W) let floor(u) select columns 0 … W−1. Near an edge, floating-point noise and pose uncertainty can change acceptance; avoid treating that boundary as ground truth.

Three ways to test the view

Before settling on projection, I weighed three approaches:

ApproachHowWhere it breaks
Angle coneAngle between the camera's forward axis and the ray to P is below a chosen half-angleAn image is a rectangle, not a circle. For a landscape image, a horizontal-FOV cone accepts points above and below the frame but misses some corners; a diagonal-FOV cone includes all corners and extends past every edge. No pixel position either.
Ground footprintCast the four corner rays onto the ground, test point-in-polygonNeeds a ground surface. Oblique shots near the horizon have corner rays that never hit the ground, and a crack on a vertical wall is not on the ground at all.
Project and bounds-checkThe function aboveRequires matching poses and intrinsics. Exact for the assumed pinhole model; returns a marker for any orientation, but does not test occlusion.

We used the third. A footprint answers a 2D question, and our users click on 3D structures.

Two practical details mattered. First, cheap tests go first: compare d·d with 1000² before rotating or dividing. That 1 km cutoff is an inspection-specific heuristic; it can exclude otherwise valid projections. In my implementation, a few thousand poses took a few milliseconds on click, on the main thread. Second, photos missing orientation or lens data used a crude cone around straight down, with no marker, ranked last and labelled approximate. A cone needs an explicit half-angle: a 45° full opening means 22.5° from its axis. These guesses stay separate from projected matches.

Distance, then centrality

Surveys overlap heavily, so a single click often matches a dozen photos. The requested order was nearest first by Euclidean camera-to-point distance, then most central among near-equals. That is a browsing preference, not a guarantee of sharpness or useful surface detail. Centrality is the distance from the centre measured in the image's own proportions and scaled so the centre is 0 and a corner is 1:

a      = H / W
centre = hypot(u/W − 0.5, (v/H − 0.5)·a) / hypot(0.5, 0.5·a)

Multiplying the vertical term by the aspect ratio makes it equivalent to measuring in pixels; without it a 3:2 image treats the top edge and the side edge as equally far from the centre.

The tricky bit is "near-equal". A comparator that says "within 0.5 m counts as a tie" is not transitive: A ties B, B ties C, but A beats C. Sort algorithms assume transitivity, and the list order ends up depending on input order. I quantise distance into bands and sort lexicographically. Distances must be finite and nonnegative, centrality finite, and photo IDs unique:

interface Match { id: string; distance: number; centre: number }

const BAND_M = 0.5;
const band = (m: Match) => Math.floor(m.distance / BAND_M);

function rank(matches: Match[]): Match[] {
  return [...matches].sort(
    (a, b) =>
      band(a) - band(b) ||        // nearer band first
      a.centre - b.centre ||      // then more central
      a.distance - b.distance ||  // then exact distance
      (a.id < b.id ? -1 : a.id > b.id ? 1 : 0), // deterministic final tie
  );
}

Here is how that plays out with the same lens (4000 × 3000, 90°), all cameras pointing straight down with image tops facing North, P on the ground at the origin. Pixels are rounded for display; ranks use unrounded values:

Camera (E, N, height)DistanceBandPixelCentreRank
B (0, 0, 100)100.00 m200(2000, 1500)0.001
E (0, −10, 100.4)100.90 m201(2000, 1301)0.082
D (30, 0, 96)100.58 m201(1375, 1500)0.253

E is about 32 cm further than D but wins because both fall in band 201 and E is more central. F is rejected because P is 80 m North of it: |y/z| = 0.8 exceeds the vertical half-tangent 1500/2000 = 0.75. The honest cost of bands shows at their edges: a camera at 100.49 m (band 200) beats a dead-centre camera at 100.51 m (band 201). I accepted that. The bands express that tradeoff explicitly; the ID tie-break keeps even exact ties independent of input order.

"In view" is not "visible"

Everything so far answers one question: does the ray from P land inside the frame? It says nothing about what sits between the camera and P. A photo of the back of a wall projects the crack on the front perfectly well.

We shipped without an occlusion test and documented the limitation. Opening a photo lets the reviewer check whether the spot is visible. I would add a cautious second pass:

  • Run it after the frustum test, only for the dozen or so matches, not every pose.
  • Raycast the mesh from each matching camera towards P. A hit closer than |P − C| − ε suggests a blocker; choose ε from reconstruction uncertainty and feature scale. A depth buffer must come from that photo’s camera and be converted to comparable ray distances; the current viewer’s buffer is insufficient.
  • Flag, don't drop. Reconstructed models have holes and floating noise, so a "blocked" verdict is itself uncertain. Mark the row "possibly hidden" and push it down the list.

Marker accuracy has limits too. Ignoring distortion can cause larger errors near the edges; an incorrect principal point shifts markers throughout the image. The size depends on calibration and pose quality. Use calibrated f_x, f_y, c_x, c_y when available. For raw photos, apply the matching forward distortion model to x/z, y/z before converting to pixels. For undistorted photos, use their adjusted intrinsics without reapplying distortion. Neither fixes pose or reconstruction errors, and this marker is no basis for millimetre measurements.

Takeaways

  • The pinhole model is one division. Everything hard is in getting the point into camera coordinates with the right axes and the right rotation direction.
  • Pick a camera convention that matches pixels. x right, y down, z forward means camera y and image rows grow together, with no sign flip to forget.
  • Check z > 0 before you divide. Otherwise points behind the camera reappear mirrored.
  • Project with explicit assumptions. Bounds-checking is exact for the assumed pinhole model and supplies the marker location; image preprocessing must match.
  • Make ties transitive. Distance bands plus explicit tie-breaks give a reproducible order.
  • Write tests with answers you can see. Before any real data, I wrote five or six cases like "nadir, North-up, point 20 m East should land right of centre on the middle row". A sign error shows up instantly when the dot lands on the wrong side.
  • Name your limits. In view is not visible. Saying so up front costs nothing and sets the right expectations.
