What Changed Between Two Drone Flights?

2026-10-23 · 12 min read

  • change detection
  • statistics
  • gis

Two drone flights can make an untouched yard look busy: red here, blue there, centimetres of apparent change. Subtracting their surfaces is easy. Deciding which differences deserve attention takes more care. I am designing a feature to compare two point-cloud versions, estimate the noise in that pair, and turn changes above a chosen threshold into cut/fill volumes. Nothing here has shipped; this is the design, including the limits I want users to see.

Unchanged ground rarely reads zero

Step on a scale twice and it might read 60.1 kg, then 59.9 kg. That 200 g difference need not be weight loss. Drone surveys have similar ambiguity: photo geometry changes, GNSS errors shift positions, grass moves. Even untouched ground produces differences, sometimes with a systematic offset. Paint every non-zero cell and the map fills with distracting specks. I need to answer three questions: how do I compare the clouds, how large is the noise for this pair, and which differences should I report?

Step 1: share a surface grid

The flights sample different spots, so their points have no direct correspondence. For this design, I use one shared grid and assign each cell one height per flight. This Digital Surface Model (DSM) represents the upper surface, including roofs, trees and parked trucks.

Rasterizing collects the points in each cell of side r and reduces their heights to one number. I am considering max for the upper surface, but it amplifies high outliers and can rise with point density even without change. I would filter outliers and compare reducers on real pairs. Empty cells are nodata, never zero: a missing observation must not become a deep pit.

The shared grid needs checking. Cell (i, j) must cover the same ground in both DSMs; a half-cell shift at a 3 m roof edge can invent 3 m of change. I would rasterize both clouds with one recipe, checking dimensions, transform, coordinate reference system (CRS), height datum and metre units, and converting nodata to NaN. Matching grids do not align misregistered surveys: horizontal offsets create false height changes on slopes. Registration needs independent stable surfaces or control.

Cell size trades coverage against detail. At 200 points/m², a 0.05 m cell holds 0.5 points on average; a 0.2 m cell averages 8 points, reducing gaps but blurring edges. Clustering still leaves holes.

Step 2: fix the sign

Δz = z_new − z_old. Positive Δz means the surface rose: fill / deposition. Negative Δz means it dropped: cut / erosion. A cell is valid only when both DSMs have a value there.

The difference raster is a DEM of Difference (DoD); here its inputs are DSMs. A sign flip turns fill into cut in someone's report. I would test known rises and falls through the job, API and display: "add material, fill must stay positive".

2.5D limit. One height per cell cannot describe vertical faces or overhangs; a wall moving sideways can show apparent rise and fall at its edges. The DoD suits top-down questions: stockpiles, excavation, earthworks.

Step 3: estimate noise on stable ground

The obvious noise measure is the standard deviation of Δz. But the DoD holds the noise and the change you are looking for, and squaring lets a 3 m stockpile dominate the estimate. An inflated threshold can hide the change it should reveal. I use a robust alternative:

  1. Median m: the middle sorted value, or the mean of the central two for an even sample. Less sensitive to isolated extremes than the mean.
  2. MAD (median absolute deviation): median(|Δz − m|). At least half the values lie within m ± MAD.
  3. NMAD = 1.4826 · MAD: scaled to estimate σ for normal noise, with sampling uncertainty.

Where does 1.4826 come from? For a normal population, 50% of values fall within approximately μ ± 0.6745σ. Here 0.6745 is the rounded 75th percentile of the standard normal. The population median equals μ, so MAD ≈ 0.6745σ and σ ≈ 1.4826 · MAD. Forget the factor and your threshold comes out a third too low.

The median has a breakdown point of about 50%: roughly half the sample must be corrupted to move it arbitrarily far. NMAD can still be distorted well below that fraction. I would estimate it on stable areas known not to change, covering representative slopes and surface types. A flat yard alone cannot describe steep, vegetated ground. Whole-map NMAD is a labelled fallback.

import numpy as np

def nmad(dz: np.ndarray) -> float:
    """Robust spread of a DoD in metres; NaN marks nodata."""
    v = np.asarray(dz, dtype=np.float64)
    v = v[np.isfinite(v)]
    if v.size == 0:
        raise ValueError("No valid observations")
    m = np.median(v)
    return 1.4826 * float(np.median(np.abs(v - m)))

def suggest_lod(dz, stable_mask, k=1.96, floor_m=0.05, step_m=0.01):
    """Apply the floor, then round UP to the slider step."""
    if not all(np.isfinite(x) and x > 0 for x in (k, floor_m, step_m)):
        raise ValueError("Parameters must be finite and positive")
    dz, mask = np.asarray(dz), np.asarray(stable_mask)
    if mask.dtype != np.bool_ or mask.shape != dz.shape:
        raise ValueError("Stable mask must be boolean and match dz")
    sigma = nmad(dz[mask])
    if sigma == 0:
        raise ValueError("Zero spread: review sampling or quantization")
    steps = np.ceil(max(k * sigma, floor_m) / step_m)
    return float(steps * step_m), sigma
Pitfall: never auto-subtract the median. It is tempting to treat median(Δz) as a GNSS height offset and remove it. But if 80% of the site was genuinely raised by 1 m, the median is +1 m, and subtracting it erases that fill and invents cut on the untouched 20%, while NMAD stays unchanged. I would report the stable-area median alongside NMAD: spread cannot reveal a constant bias. Removing an offset requires evidence from independently verified stable surfaces or control, with the correction recorded.

Step 4: choose a detection threshold

The level of detection (LoD) is the smallest |Δz| I am willing to call change: LoD = k · σ before applying the floor and rounding, with σ estimated by NMAD. A cell counts as change when |Δz| ≥ LoD. Choosing k is choosing between two errors. For zero-centred normal noise with known σ, this is the expected share of truly unchanged cells that still cross ±kσ:

Even k = 1.96 paints 1 in 20 stable cells; a higher k cleans the map but eats the gentle edge of a real pile. My first draft used a fixed 5 cm. With 4 cm of noise that is only 1.25σ, and about 21% of stable cells light up. I would suggest a pair-specific LoD on a 5 cm to 1 m slider in 1 cm steps. The 5 cm floor is a design choice, not a measured accuracy guarantee. A suggestion above 1 m needs review, not silent clipping. These rates describe individual cells, not the probability a highlighted cell is real change. With heavy tails, I would check stable-area exceedances directly.

For independent surface errors, σ_Δ = √(σ₁² + σ₂²): two 3 cm surfaces give about 4.2 cm. With correlated errors, σ_Δ² = σ₁² + σ₂² − 2 Cov(e₁, e₂). Stable-area DoD NMAD estimates the combined spread directly; do not add another √2 factor. It still misses shared absolute errors and may not represent noisier parts of the map.

Over all 25 cells, real change included, the standard deviation jumps to 0.213 m (about 5.7 times the stable-area standard deviation) while NMAD rises only to 0.059 m: robust but not immune, since 9 of 25 cells are real change. (Nine cells only show the mechanics. Even a large sample needs spatial coverage: neighbouring errors are correlated.)

Threshold. 1.96 × 0.029652 ≈ 0.0581 m, rounded up to the 1 cm step: LoD = 0.06 m. Six cells pass as fill and four as cut. The +0.05 cell, maybe the toe of the pile, falls just short: the price of a threshold.

Step 5: sum volumes with bins

Each cell is the base of a column of height |Δz|. For square cells in a suitable metric projection, A = r². Volume sums the full retained differences; it does not subtract the threshold from each height:

V_fill = A · Σ Δz over cells with Δz ≥ t

V_cut = A · Σ |Δz| over cells with Δz ≤ −t

V_net = V_fill − V_cut

In the example, fill = 0.25 × 2.07 = 0.5175 m³ and cut = 0.25 × 0.87 = 0.2175 m³ before cleanup. On unchanged ground, symmetric noise inflates expected fill and cut equally. The net can look right while both gross volumes are too high.

The slider must update volumes instantly, but the browser does not hold tens of millions of Δz values. So the job writes a histogram with sums: per bin index k, the cell count C[k] and the sum of Δz, S[k]. With 1 cm bins and thresholds t = n · 1 cm:

V_fill(n) = A · Σ S[k] for k ≥ n

V_cut(n) = A · |Σ S[k]| for k ≤ −n

area_fill(n) = A · Σ C[k] for k ≥ n

Whole bins give the retained sums without bin-centre approximation, apart from floating-point summation error. Boundary handling still needs a contract: 0.29 / 0.01 evaluates to 28.999999999999996. Below, EPS admits values within about 10⁻¹¹ m below a boundary. Every mask must use the same bin rule; I would check both languages with shared binary64 fixtures. The browser consumes stored bins. This convention is numerical tolerance, not survey precision.

type Bin = { count: number; sumDz: number };
const W = 0.01;   // metres per slider step
const K = 100;    // maximum threshold index; tail bins at +/-K
const EPS = 1e-9; // in bin units, equivalent to 1e-11 m

export const binIndex = (dz: number) =>
  Math.sign(dz) * Math.min(K, Math.floor(Math.abs(dz) / W + EPS));

export function buildHistogram(dz: Float64Array): Map<number, Bin> {
  const h = new Map<number, Bin>();
  for (const v of dz) {
    if (!Number.isFinite(v)) continue; // nodata is skipped, never 0
    const k = binIndex(v);
    const b = h.get(k) ?? { count: 0, sumDz: 0 };
    b.count += 1; b.sumDz += v;
    h.set(k, b);
  }
  return h;
}

export function volumes(h: Map<number, Bin>, n: number, cellArea: number) {
  if (!Number.isInteger(n) || n < 1 || n > K ||
      !Number.isFinite(cellArea) || cellArea <= 0) {
    throw new RangeError("Invalid threshold index or cell area");
  }
  let fill = 0, cut = 0;
  for (const [k, b] of h) {
    if (k >= n) fill += b.sumDz; else if (k <= -n) cut -= b.sumDz;
  }
  return { fill: fill * cellArea, cut: cut * cellArea, net: (fill - cut) * cellArea };
}

The ±K bins retain counts and original sums for all tail values. They remain valid at every supported positive threshold. Prefix/suffix sums can make slider lookups constant-time.

Step 6: filter small regions

Some noise forms isolated specks; some real change does too. I would group the cells of each mask into connected components (8-connectivity: edge or corner neighbours) and drop regions below a minimum area, given in m² and converted with N = ⌈area / A⌉. Fill and cut use separate masks, so touching opposite signs never merge. Corner contact joins regions under this rule; missing cells do not.

In the example, 0.5 m² means N = 2 cells. Fill forms one 6-cell region and survives. Cut forms a 3-cell region plus the lone −0.09 cell, which is removed: the same noisy cell that inflated the standard deviation. After cleanup:

Two volume totals. The slider total comes from the histogram, before region filtering; per-region volumes come after. Here they differ by 0.0225 m³ on the cut side. Label both. A histogram loses cell positions, so it cannot recompute region filtering when the threshold changes; the job must rerun it or serve precomputed results. A minimum region also deletes real change smaller than it, like a 0.3 m² pothole: choose it from the smallest object users care about.

What I'd show the user

  • A diverging colour ramp centred on zero: warm for fill, cool for cut, grey where |Δz| < LoD. A legend is mandatory and says "new − old".
  • The threshold slider, pre-set to the suggested LoD: "NMAD 3.0 cm on stable area, LoD 6 cm", alongside the stable-area median, valid sample count and coverage.
  • Volumes with qualified uncertainty. On a fixed N-cell footprint with equal error standard deviation σ, independent noise gives volume standard deviation A·√N·σ; perfect positive correlation gives the upper limit A·N·σ. Using NMAD as σ gives 0.25 × 6 × 0.030 = 0.045 m³ in that limiting model. I would label this a noise scale, not a confidence interval: selection by threshold, bias and varying errors need separate treatment. A constant offset shifts an unthresholded fixed-area net volume by offset × area (2 cm over 1,000 m² is 20 m³).
  • Nodata area, separately. Within the comparison footprint, cells missing from either flight are "unknown", not "unchanged".
  • Plain caveats. A newly parked truck counts as fill.

To calibrate k, I would use separate repeat flights over verified unchanged ground. Reprocessing identical photos only tests processing repeatability and may return identical surfaces. With a fixed region-size rule, under about 2% surviving area is an initial acceptance target, not a confidence level. I would validate on held-out null pairs and synthetic pile-and-pit pairs, checking missed change as well as false alarms.

Takeaways

  • Use aligned surfaces: one recipe, one shared grid, nodata stays nodata.
  • Fix the sign once (new − old, positive = fill) and test it.
  • Estimate spread with NMAD on stable ground. Remember the 1.4826, and check bias separately.
  • A threshold is a trade-off. Derive it (k · NMAD), show it, let people move it.
  • Count and sum per integer bin preserve retained volumes at supported thresholds; share the boundary rule.
  • Never silently "fix" a vertical offset.

The arithmetic is small; the assumptions carry the weight. I want users to see the threshold, its evidence and its limits, so a clear map does not promise more certainty than the surveys can support.
